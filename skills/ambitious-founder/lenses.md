# Board lenses

A board member applies ONE lens and never speaks as the person a framework comes
from. Lenses 1-3 are short summaries, in our own words, of published frameworks
(from the MIT-licensed founder-skill by Jake Schincariol). Lens 4 is original to
this skill, added for physical products; it is not attributed to anyone.

## 1. The Offers lens

Source: Alex Hormozi, *$100M Offers* (2021).

**Pick the market first.** The best offer fails in a bad market. Score the market
on four things: the pain is massive, the buyers have money to spend, they are
easy to find and reach, and the market is growing. A weak market is the most
common reason this lens passes.

**The value equation.** How much a buyer values an offer goes up with the dream
outcome and the perceived likelihood of getting it, and down with the time it
takes and the effort and sacrifice it asks. Score each of the four. Most offers
fail on likelihood (no proof) or effort (too much asked of the buyer).

**Do not compete on price.** If the only difference is being cheaper, it is a
commodity. Ask what would make this so good that people feel stupid saying no:
more of the outcome, faster, with less effort, with a guarantee.

**Build the stack.** List every problem the buyer hits on the way to the outcome,
turn each into a solution, keep the ones that are cheap to deliver and valuable
to the buyer, and stack them. Then strengthen it with real scarcity or urgency
(never fake), bonuses, a guarantee and a clear name.

Questions: Who exactly has this pain and money? Why would they believe it works?
How fast do they get a result? What is the guarantee? Could you double the price
and still be the obvious choice?

## 2. The Monopoly lens

Source: Peter Thiel with Blake Masters, *Zero to One* (2014).

**Competition destroys profit.** The goal is a small market you can dominate,
then expand from.

**What makes a durable business:** proprietary technology or know-how roughly ten
times better on something that matters; network effects; economies of scale; a
brand. Most small businesses have none, so ask which one this could build.

**The seven questions:** engineering (breakthrough or incremental?), timing (why
now?), monopoly (big share of a small market?), people (right team?),
distribution (a way to reach buyers, not just a product?), durability (still
holding in ten years?), and the secret (what does the founder know that others
do not?).

**Distribution is half of it.** Ask how the first 100 customers arrive and what
each one costs.

Questions: Who else sells this within reach of the same buyer? What small market
can this own first? What is the secret? What stops a copycat in month six?

## 3. The Product lens

Source: Walter Isaacson, *Steve Jobs* (2011), and the public record of how Apple
built products.

**Focus means saying no.** Cut until what is left is the one thing the business
must do brilliantly.

**Start from the experience and work back.** Describe the moment the customer
uses the product, then work back to what has to be true to deliver it.

**Own the whole experience.** Every touchpoint, from the first ad to the receipt
to the follow-up, is the product. Seams are where it breaks.

**Taste and craft, including the parts nobody sees.**

**Simplicity is the hard part.** If the pitch needs a paragraph, the product is
not clear yet.

Questions: What is the one thing? What do the first five minutes feel like? What
would you cut? Where is the experience handed between people or systems, and what
goes wrong there?

## 4. The Make lens (physical products only; original to this skill)

Use only when the business sells a physical product. Skip for pure services or
software.

**The BOM is the price floor.** Landed unit cost = parts + assembly + test +
packaging + freight + duty + yield loss. Ask for the BOM at the planned volume,
not at prototype quantities.

**Tooling and NRE are startup cash.** Injection moulds, dies, fixtures,
certification (e.g. CE / FCC / UL / safety marks where relevant) and the first
production run are paid before the first sale. Ask what MOQ the suppliers set and
how much cash that ties up.

**Design for manufacture and assembly.** Fewer parts, fewer fasteners, one
assembly direction, standard stock, tolerances only where the function needs
them. Ask which feature is the hardest to make and whether it is needed.

**Supply risk.** Single-source parts, long lead times, allocation-prone
electronics. Ask what happens if one supplier slips eight weeks.

**Returns and warranty.** Field failure rate times the cost of a return comes off
the margin. Ask what fails first and how that was tested.

Questions: What is the landed cost at 1k and at 10k units? What does tooling cost
and when is it paid? Which part is hardest to make? What has been physically
tested, and against which load or standard?
